The scroll is 206 by 103 centimetres, ink and light colour on silk, and it hangs in the National Palace Museum in Taipei. Most of it is one mountain: a wall of rock that begins about a third of the way up and does not stop. Beneath it, a band of mist that explains nothing. Beneath that, trees, a track, and — once somebody points them out — a mule train and two men, each roughly a centimetre tall.

Fan Kuan painted it around the year 1000. It is, as far as anyone can tell, the only work of his that survives.

## The case for calling it sublime

Burke published his *Philosophical Enquiry* in 1757 and gave the sublime a recipe: vastness, obscurity, power, and a certain useful terror. Kant refined it in 1790. The mathematical sublime, in his account, is what happens when something is so large that the imagination fails to hold it in a single view. The failure is the point. In failing, you discover that you possess a faculty — reason — that can think what the senses cannot take in, and the feeling that follows is not really about the mountain. It is a kind of self-respect.

Fan Kuan supplies the whole recipe. The scale defeats you. The mist obscures exactly the part that would let you measure the rock against anything. The waterfall on the right is a single white thread, so thin it reads as distance rather than water. And the travelers are there to be missed, so that finding them re-scales the picture around you in one unpleasant lurch.

By any formal test, this is the sublime.

## Where it stops working

Except that Kant is specific about the conditions. The sublime requires that you be safe. You have to be watching from somewhere — a fixed point, outside the thing, in no actual danger. The whole experience depends on a subject standing apart from an object and surviving the encounter.

Fan Kuan will not give you that position. Neither will any Northern Song landscape, and the reason is technical rather than mystical.

European painting of the following centuries was built on a single station point. Linear perspective assumes one eye, in one place, at one moment. It is a very effective machine for producing exactly the confrontation Kant describes, because it puts you *here* and the mountain *there* and holds you both still.

Chinese landscape was not built that way. It is not quite landscape, either: the genre is 山水, *shanshui*, mountain-and-water. Not land, which lies under your feet and ends at a horizon, but two things held in relation, with you somewhere among them.

Guo Xi — a court painter working about seventy years after Fan Kuan — set out the alternative in *Linquan Gaozhi*, the treatise his son Guo Si assembled around 1080. He calls it the three distances:

> 自山下而仰山巔，謂之高遠；自山前而窺山後，謂之深遠；自近山而望遠山，謂之平遠。
>
> Looking up from the foot of the mountain to its peak: high distance. Peering from the front of the mountain to what lies behind it: deep distance. Gazing from a near mountain toward a far one: level distance.

These are three different places to stand, and a painting is expected to use more than one. Your eye is not positioned; it is routed. You enter low among the trees, follow the track, lose it in the mist, and arrive at the summit without ever having occupied a spot from which the whole thing could be seen at once.

So the Kantian moment never arrives — not because the painting is gentler, but because there is no stable "you" for the mountain to overwhelm. The imagination is never asked to hold the scene in one view, so it never fails, so reason never rides in to compensate. Something else happens instead.

## What it is for

Guo Xi is unusually direct about what he wants a landscape to do:

> 世之篤論，謂山水有可行者，有可望者，有可遊者，有可居者……但可行可望不如可居可遊之為得。
>
> It is well said that some landscapes can be walked through, some looked at, some wandered in, and some lived in. But those one can walk through or look at are not the equal of those one can wander in and live in.

*Walked through* and *looked at* are the tourist's verbs. Guo Xi ranks them last. The painting is not there to be confronted; it is there to be inhabited, and he means this practically — he goes on to complain that in real country, land you could actually live in is maybe three or four parts in ten, which is precisely why a painting should supply it.

This is a different destination from the sublime. Burke's mountain leaves you rattled and Kant's leaves you enlarged. Guo Xi's leaves you resident. Read the travelers again with that in mind and they stop being a device for registering scale. They are just people on a road, going somewhere, on a working day, under a mountain that is not paying attention to them. The picture is not staging their insignificance. It is showing you the road.

He also thought the viewer had to bring something:

> 看山水亦有體，以林泉之心臨之則價高，以驕侈之目臨之則價低。
>
> There is a discipline to looking at landscape. Approach it with the heart of forests and streams and its worth is high; approach it with an arrogant and extravagant eye and its worth is low.

Which is a claim you can dislike — it makes the work's value contingent on the state of the person in front of it — but it is at least honest about what it is asking. The sublime asks nothing of you except that you show up and be startled. This asks you to have prepared.

## The signature

For most of a thousand years nobody could prove Fan Kuan painted it. The attribution was old and confident and unevidenced.

Then on 5 August 1958, Li Lincan, working at the National Palace Museum, found two characters in the lower right of the scroll — 范寬 — tucked into the leaves of a tree just past the mule train, drawn thin enough to pass for branches. They had been there the whole time.

I keep returning to where he put them. Not on the rock face. Not in the empty upper register where a colophon would sit. Down in the undergrowth, at the scale of the two men and the mules, on the near side of the mist — in the part of the painting you can walk through.

*Travelers Among Mountains and Streams* is in the National Palace Museum, Taipei. Guo Xi's own surviving masterwork, Early Spring, dated 1072, is in the same collection.
