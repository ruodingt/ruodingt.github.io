In 2008 Jimmy Chin, Conrad Anker and Renan Ozturk spent seventeen days on the Shark's Fin, a wall of rock and ice on Meru in the Garhwal Himalaya that nobody had climbed. A storm pinned them in a portaledge for the better part of a week. They ran out of food. They kept going, and then, about a hundred metres below the top, they turned around and went down.

They came back three years later and finished it.

The story is usually told as a story about the second trip. I think it is a story about the first one, and about the fact that Chin later called his book of photographs *There and Back*.

## The shape of the problem

Transcendence means going past. Past the ordinary, past the body's limits, past the self that keeps insisting on its own importance. Every tradition that takes the idea seriously builds a machine for producing it — the summit, the fast, the retreat, the drug, the vigil — and every one of those machines fails in one of two ways.

The first is that you get stuck up there.

Plenty of people transcend; that was never the hard part. The failure is that having got past the ordinary world, some of them decline to come back, and mistake this for an achievement. You can recognise the type. Everything ordinary has been seen through. Nothing is simply itself any more; it is a construct, a conditioning, a symptom, a veil. They are not wrong, exactly. They are just extremely difficult to have lunch with.

The second failure is harder to see, because from outside it looks like health. These people come back every time. They just need a bigger mountain each time.

The sublime is habit-forming, and like anything habit-forming it has a tolerance curve. The route that hollowed you out last year returns a smaller amount this year. So the wall gets steeper, the retreat gets longer, the fast gets harder, the dose goes up — and the ordinary world, which was supposed to be the destination, quietly becomes the interval between doses. You are technically coming home. You are not actually here.

An escalating series has no natural stopping point. That is the whole trouble with it. There is no biggest mountain, no final vastness that settles the matter, and a sequence of this kind cannot be completed by going further along it — it can only be stepped off. In the specific case of alpinism, everyone in the sport can name where the series terminates if nobody steps off.

So transcendence has to be applied to itself. Whatever the view from up there was worth, the two things it is not worth are staying, and going back for more.

## Seeing the mountain

Chan Buddhism said this early and said it better than anyone since. Qingyuan Weixin, an eleventh-century monk, described his own progress in three lines:

> 老僧三十年前未參禪時，見山是山，見水是水。及至後來親見知識，有箇入處，見山不是山，見水不是水。而今得箇休歇處，依前見山祇是山，見水祇是水。
>
> Thirty years ago, before I took up Chan, I saw mountains as mountains and rivers as rivers. Later, when I came to know something, and found a way in, I saw that mountains are not mountains and rivers are not rivers. Now that I have found a place to rest, I see mountains as only mountains and rivers as only rivers.

The trap is in the middle. The second stage — *mountains are not mountains* — feels like arrival. It is dramatic, it is hard-won, and it comes with the distinct sensation of having been let in on something. It is also, in his telling, not the end. The end is a mountain that is only a mountain.

Note what this does to anyone watching. The third stage is behaviourally indistinguishable from the first. The person who has been all the way round and the person who never left both say that a mountain is a mountain. There is no way to display the difference, and no credit available for it. Whatever else Qingyuan was describing, he was describing a destination with no view.

## What awe is for

Psychology has a working definition, and it is more useful than it sounds. Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt proposed in 2003 that awe requires two things: **perceived vastness** — something too large for you, whether a view or an idea — and a **need for accommodation**, meaning the experience cannot be filed under anything you already have, so the filing system itself has to change.

Set that second condition next to Qingyuan. *Mountains are not mountains* is a need for accommodation. It is the sound of a schema failing under load. Awe is the engine of the middle stage, and the middle stage is not optional — there is no route to the third view that goes around the wreckage of the first.

But the same literature reports something else. Awe is self-diminishing: it points at the thing, not at you. It reliably produces what researchers call the **small self** — reduced self-focus, an increased sense of being one part of something large — and people who have just felt it treat strangers better.

This is where awe parts company with the sublime as Kant described it. His mountain ends in a kind of self-respect: the mind discovers a faculty in itself that outmatches anything nature can put in front of it. Awe, measured rather than deduced, runs the other way. It makes you smaller.

Which gives a usable test. Where awe is doing its work, the quantity that shrinks is the self. Where it has become a habit, the quantity that grows is the dose. Two people can report the same mountain and the same feeling, and you can tell them apart by asking which number has been going up.

## The two pictures after the void

The Song monk Kuoan Shiyuan made this structural. His *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures* map the whole path: searching for the ox, finding its tracks, catching it, taming it, riding it home. By the eighth picture, man and ox are both forgotten, and the picture is an empty circle:

> 鞭索人牛盡屬空，碧天遼闊信難通。
>
> Whip, rope, man and ox — all of it empty. The blue sky is vast, and no message gets through.

That is transcendence, cleanly achieved, and it is where a lesser tradition would have stopped. Eight is a good number to stop at. The circle is the most quotable image in the sequence.

Kuoan puts two more pictures after it. The ninth returns to the source, where water is simply water and the flowers are simply red. And the tenth, 入廛垂手 — entering the marketplace with open hands:

> 露胸跣足入廛來，抹土塗灰笑滿腮。不用神仙真秘訣，直教枯木放花開。
>
> Barechested and barefoot, he comes into the market, smeared with dust and ash, grinning. No immortal's secret formula — he just makes the dead tree bloom.

The man in the last picture has done every stage of the work and looks like nobody. Dust, bare feet, a grin. He is in a market, among people buying fish.

Notice that this is the only way the series could have ended. A sequence that escalates cannot be closed by escalating — there is always one more circle to draw, one more emptiness beyond the last one. It can only be closed by an anticlimax, and Kuoan chose the largest anticlimax available to him. There is nothing sublime about a fish stall. That is precisely why it goes last.

The sequence was never a ladder. It was a loop, and the last rung is the ground.

## Why down is the harder half

In climbing the argument is not metaphorical. Most accidents happen on the descent, when the objective is gone and the body has already spent what it had. Ed Viesturs, who climbed all fourteen eight-thousanders without supplemental oxygen, reduced it to a rule: getting to the top is optional, getting down is mandatory.

What makes it hard is that the descent pays nothing. The summit hands you everything at once — the adrenaline, the story, the photograph, the moment when the effort finally has a shape. The way down offers no shape at all. You are tired, the weather is turning, the light is going, and at the bottom there is only the ordinary, which is exactly what you left.

Which is why it is the real test, and why traditions that are serious about transcendence spend most of their instruction on the return. Anyone can be moved. Staying moved is not the achievement it feels like.

I wrote [last time](/2026/08/10/the-sublime-in-chinese-landscape-painting.html) about Guo Xi ranking the landscapes you can live in above the landscapes you can only look at. It would suit this essay to say that shanshui therefore has no interest in summits, and that would be false: Northern Song landscape is built around its dominant peak, which Guo Xi calls the ruler that the other mountains attend.

The tradition is doing something less tidy than a descent. It is not offering a route back from the vast — it is denying that you need to set out. Guo Xi is explicit about why a painted landscape is worth having at all. The gentleman longs for streams and rocks, but will not leave his ruler and his parents in order to go and find them. So:

> 不下堂筵，坐窮泉壑。
>
> Without leaving your seat at the table, you exhaust the springs and ravines.

That is transcendence with the journey deleted, and he does not mean it as a consolation prize. The post is kept, the household is kept, the obligations are kept, and the mountain hangs on the wall. Where escalation asks *how much further*, this asks why you assumed you had to go anywhere.

## There and back

Chin has said, in one form or another, across twenty years of interviews, that the goal was always to get home; that turning around and coming back alive is the right decision; that his relationship with climbing moved from chasing summits to understanding what he was in. He is not a mystic and does not talk like one. He is a man who has watched friends not come down.

*There and Back.* Two prepositions and a conjunction, and the conjunction is doing the work. Not *there*. Not a book of summits, although he has the photographs for it.

You come back to the same house. The tea is the same, the street is the same, and the mountain, seen from the kitchen window, is only a mountain.

That is the whole of it, and it is not a small thing.
